The project

Six months to fix a system people had used for years

I joined in January. The project was a proposal for the State of Missouri, planned for six months, and everyone wanted it finished by June.

The Department of Elementary and Secondary Education (DESE) uses its School Finance portal to calculate and track payments to school districts across the state. State staff run the calculations, districts check what they've been paid, and the public can look up the numbers.

The portal still worked, but it was old and hard to use. DESE wanted three things: a better-organized application, a faster and clearer interface, and full accessibility.

What I did

Research, information architecture, the design system, wireframes, prototypes and the developer handoff.

Who I worked with

State officials, technical staff who have looked after the system for about 20 years, the business analyst, the data team and the developers. I ran the workshops and design reviews, and guided the developers through the handoff.

Heuristic review

Checking the old portal against Nielsen's 10 heuristics

To turn my notes into something the team could act on, I went through the old portal against Jakob Nielsen's ten usability heuristics. It gave every problem a name and made it easier to explain to stakeholders why something needed to change, not just that it looked old.

1Visibility of system statusProblem found

Old portalYou couldn't tell whether a calculation had run without opening it.

New portalThe dashboard shows each calculation's status and last run time; Appropriation shows a check mark once saved.

2Match with the real worldPartly

Old portalSome screens used system terms instead of the words staff use every day.

New portalLabels follow DESE's own language, and line numbers match the official forms staff already know.

3User control and freedomProblem found

Old portalMoving around meant following chains of links, and it was easy to lose your place.

New portalA fixed sidebar is always visible, and detail pages have a back arrow to where you came from.

4Consistency and standardsProblem found

Old portalPages looked and behaved differently from each other.

New portalOne design system: same header, same table, same buttons and same footer on every page.

5Error preventionProblem found

Old portalDistrict and period were typed again on many pages, and connected fields changed each other without warning.

New portalDistrict is pre-filled and locked for district users, calculated values are read-only, and inputs show the expected format.

6Recognition rather than recallProblem found

Old portalPeople had to remember which page held which information.

New portalNavigation is grouped by job, and the dashboard brings the key numbers to one screen.

7Flexibility and efficiencyProblem found

Old portalNo shortcuts: calculations couldn't be generated or read in one place.

New portalQuick actions on the dashboard (run calculations, enter ADA data, generate a report).

8Aesthetic and minimalist designProblem found

Old portalAppropriation and maintenance shared one crowded table.

New portalOne job per page: separate Appropriation and Maintenance pages, with maintenance types in one sorted dropdown.

9Recognize and recover from errorsMinor

Old portalNot one of the main problems we found.

New portalMessages explain what to fix in plain words, next to the field.

10Help and documentationMinor

Old portalNot one of the main problems we found.

New portalA Help Center link in every sidebar and a DESE contact footer on every page.

Structure

Organizing the portal around who's using it

The old portal showed the same long list of links to everyone. In the new one, people pick their role when they log in and see only what they need. State staff get every tool, districts see their own district, and the public can browse published payments. A few pages, like LEA Payment Calculations and Payment Transmittal, are shared but adapt to the role. For example, a district user's district is already filled in and locked.

UX laws

The principles behind the layout

Many of the layout decisions come from well-known UX laws. Here's where each one shows up in the portal.

Hick's law

More choices take longer to decide between.

In the portal: District users see four menu items, not the full DESE list. Navigation is filtered by role.

Jakob's law

People expect a product to work like others they already use.

In the portal: A familiar admin layout: fixed sidebar on the left, top bar with alerts and the user menu.

Miller's law and chunking

People can only hold a few things in mind at once, so group them.

In the portal: Year Rollover, Appropriation and Maintenance sit together under Yearly Tasks. The dashboard leads with four numbers.

Law of common region

Things inside the same box feel like a group.

In the portal: Filters, tables and summaries each live in their own white card.

Law of similarity

Things that look alike feel related.

In the portal: Every data table has the same navy header, so once you can read one, you can read them all.

Law of proximity

Things placed close together look connected.

In the portal: Calculated values sit right next to the inputs they come from, like the ADA ratios on Estimated ADA.

Fitts's law

Big, close targets are faster to hit.

In the portal: Large quick-action tiles on the dashboard and a clear Save button at the end of each form.

Von Restorff effect

The one thing that looks different gets noticed.

In the portal: Blue is saved for actions and the active page; red is only used for high priority and alerts.

Tesler's law

Some complexity can't be removed, only moved.

In the portal: The system carries it: the district is filled in, calculations run from one button, and results show their status.

Design system

One document, then Figma and code from it

With one designer, six months and a portal this connected, I couldn't design every screen by hand. So in February I wrote the design document first and built the system from it: tokens and components in Figma, and matching React components for the developers. The document was the reference for both.

Design document

Colors, typography, spacing and layout, core components, page flow for each role, key pages, responsive behavior and accessibility. Version 1.0 is dated May 29, 2026.

Figma library

Color tokens taken straight from the document, 18 components plus a dashboard layout shell, and one page structure (Header and Body) on every screen.

React components

Built with Claude Code from the same tokens in React, TypeScript and Tailwind CSS, with no outside UI library. Developers got components that matched the Figma file.

Tokens

Every color has one job

The document lists each color with what it's for: navy for headers and table headers, blue for links, the active page and focus rings, yellow for alerts. The Figma tokens and the code use the same values, so nobody picks a color by eye.

Component properties

One component, three roles

The sidebar and filter bar have a Role property: DESE staff see every control, district users see their district pre-filled and locked, and the public sees read-only dropdowns. Three views, one component set.

Accessibility

Built into the components

A visible focus ring on every interactive element, links always underlined, inputs that show the expected format, and calculated values shown read-only. Each screen gets these from the components, not from a final check.

Review

What I checked myself

I compared everything AI produced against the design document and the accessibility rules before it went to stakeholders or developers. The structure, wording and priorities came from the research and reviews, not from the tool.

What writing it down first changed

Decisions were settled in words before they were drawn, so reviews could focus on the portal instead of colors and spacing. And because the developers' components came from the same tokens, the handoff could focus on behavior instead of matching pixels.

Inside the Figma file

How the file was built

I set up the Figma file so it could grow without falling apart. Each workflow has its own section on the canvas. Every screen uses the same structure: a Header component and a Body (margin) frame in vertical auto layout that holds the page. Components like the sidebar and filter bar have a Role property, so one component covers DESE, district and public views.

Handoff

Handing it over

The high-fidelity prototypes went through several rounds of review with stakeholders on MS Teams calls, and I updated them after each one. At the end, I handed off the Figma file in Dev Mode and walked developers and stakeholders through it.

Alongside the file I wrote a design document, version 1.0, dated May 29, 2026, so everyone had one written reference for how the portal should look and behave.

Looking back

What this project taught me

In six months the portal went from a maze of links to a structured, role-based system with its own design system, reviewed prototypes and a full handoff package.

Listen to the long-timers

The people who had kept this system running for 20 years explained in an hour what would have taken me weeks to figure out.

Build the system first

Having the design system ready early is what made a June finish possible.

AI saves time on building, not on thinking

The tools made me faster. The decisions still came from research and conversations with real people.
